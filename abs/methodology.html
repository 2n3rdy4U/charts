Methodology & Data Quality

Auto Loan & Auto Lease ABS Data Explorers ← Loan Explorer  ·  ← Lease Explorer

This page documents the data sources, definitions, and quality considerations behind every chart and table in the Auto Loan and Auto Lease ABS Data Explorers. It is written for practitioners who need to evaluate whether the numbers meet their standard of care before citing them.

Sections tagged Auto Loan or Auto Lease apply to that explorer only. Everything else applies to both.

1. Data Source

Delinquency, repossession and credit-score figures are derived from SEC ABS-EE asset-level filings submitted to EDGAR under Regulation AB II, which securitization trusts are required to file monthly. Loss figures come from the trusts’ monthly Form 10-D servicer reports (section 4). We fetch, parse, deduplicate and aggregate these filings to produce the figures shown. Typical latency from a new SEC filing to the explorer is under 24 hours.

2. Active Pool & Delinquency

Both explorers report count-weighted, mutually exclusive DPD buckets: the 30–59 bucket, the 60+ bucket and the current (<30) bucket sum to the total active count and do not overlap.

  • 60+ DPD rate: active assets 60 or more days past due, divided by total active assets.
  • 30–59 DPD rate: active assets 30 to 59 days past due, same denominator.
  • 30+ DPD rate: the two combined.
  • Current rate: active assets less than 30 days past due.

A note on weighting

Delinquency is count-weighted: every active loan or lease counts once, regardless of size, so the rate answers what share of borrowers are behind. Loss is balance-weighted: it is a ratio of dollars, so it answers what share of the pool’s money was lost. Both are ratios of sums rather than averages of deal-level rates, so a small deal with an extreme rate cannot distort the issuer figure.

The two conventions are standard for their respective metrics, but they are not the same question — on a book where large loans behave differently from small ones, delinquency and loss will diverge for reasons of construction alone. Each chart names its denominator on the y-axis.

What counts as “active”

Loan and lease filings report the end of an asset’s life differently, so the two explorers apply slightly different tests:

  • Auto Loan a loan is active while it carries an outstanding balance (above a nominal $10).
  • Auto Lease a lease is active while it carries a balance and has not been reported as terminated. Lease filings mark ended leases explicitly, and issuers differ in how they mark active ones, so the test relies on the termination status rather than on any single issuer’s convention.

Auto Loan Some issuers report placeholder days-past-due values for loans that have already been charged off; those loans are excluded from the delinquency buckets.

3. Reconciliation Against Issuer Filings Auto Lease

We validate the lease active-pool definition against issuers’ own monthly investor reports (Form 10-D). Checked against a sample deal’s report, the end-of-period pool count and securitization value reproduce exactly under our definition.

Where the lease numerator diverges from issuer reports

Even with matched denominators, our 30+ DPD count typically runs 10–20% above the corresponding figure in monthly investor reports. Two structural reasons:

  • Payment extensions. When a lessee receives a payment extension during a collection period, the issuer treats the lease as cured for reporting purposes. Our ABS-EE snapshot still carries the lease at its pre-extension DPD until the following month’s filing.
  • Charge-off-pending exclusions. Issuers typically remove leases flagged for default or repossession from the delinquency table and report them separately. Our snapshot still includes those leases in the 60+ bucket while they retain a balance.

Trends and relative comparisons across issuers remain meaningful. For deal-level absolute counts, consult the issuer’s Form 10-D.

4. Loss Metrics

Loss figures come from monthly 10-D servicer distribution reports (EDGAR), not from the ABS-EE loan tape. The servicer states each deal’s losses directly, which is the authoritative pool-level figure. The Losses tab offers two views of that data.

Cumulative Net Loss, by vintage or series

  • Cumulative Net Loss Rate: cumulative net losses (charge‑offs net of recoveries, per the servicer) ÷ original pool balance, indexed by months since issuance. Note this denominator differs from the annualized measure below, which divides by the average outstanding balance.
  • Months since issuance count from a deal’s first servicer report, whatever period that report covers: the first report is month 1 and each later report is dated by its collection month, so a missing report leaves a gap rather than shifting what follows. One rule for every issuer, so deals line up on the same axis. (Some servicers number their own reports differently; those numbers are not used.)
  • View: selectable — By Vintage (pool‑weighted across deals of the same issuance year) or By Series (individual deals).

Annualized Net Loss, by calendar month

CNL is cumulative and indexed by loan age, so it cannot be placed on a calendar axis: averaging it across an issuer’s deals in a given month produces a figure driven by pool age mix rather than credit. The annualized net loss rate is a point-in-time flow, so it can be aggregated per issuer over calendar time the way the delinquency rate is.

  • Formula: 12 × net losses for the period ÷ average outstanding pool balance (the mean of beginning and ending balance), summed across an issuer’s deals in each collection month — the month the figures describe (a September servicer report covers August collections). Each month stands alone: the rate is not smoothed or averaged across months.
  • Basis: the All pools / Seasoned (>6 mo) toggle mirrors the Delinquency tab. A young deal books almost no losses, so an issuer writing new deals looks better than it is on the all-pools basis.
  • Below zero is a net gain, not a negative loss. The 10-D line item is literally “Principal Net Loss / (Gain)”: a month whose recoveries on previously charged-off receivables exceed its gross charge-offs books a gain, which the filings print in accounting parentheses. Prime shelves cross below zero routinely.

Because the two tabs read different filings, their as-of dates can differ. Both are dated by the month the figures describe, but servicer reports are filed around the middle of the following month, so a shelf’s newest loss month can sit one month behind its newest delinquency month. The loss chart states its as-of month in the subtitle. A shelf whose 10-D figures fail our internal reconciliation check is withheld from the annualized series rather than published unverified.

Lease shelves: credit losses and residual value Auto Lease

A lease pool can lose money two ways: a lessee defaults (a credit loss), or a returned vehicle sells for less than the residual value written into the lease (a residual loss). Servicers report the two separately, and so do we.

  • Net credit loss follows the loan definitions above — cumulative by months since issuance, annualized by collection month, reconciled and gated the same way — as a percentage of the securitization value (the present value of the remaining lease payments plus the securitized residual), which is the pool measure a lease trust reports.
  • Cumulative residual value gain (loss) is the servicer’s cumulative gain or loss on vehicles returned at the end of their leases, as a percentage of the original securitization value; a gain is positive. It is cumulative by months since issuance and is the larger of the two risks on a prime lease pool.
  • The two are not added into one “total loss”: only some servicers report such a figure, and their definitions differ.

5. Repossessions (First-Time Repossession Rate) Auto Loan

The monthly rate at which loans are repossessed for the first time, from the repossession flag in the loan-level filings.

  • Event: a loan’s first repossession — the first month its repossession flag is set. A loan counts once, ever. If the vehicle is later redeemed and the loan reinstated, the repossession still counts: it happened.
  • Numerator: first repossessions in the month among loans reported at the start of it, whatever their status then — including repossessions of loans already charged off, which some servicers carry out routinely after charge-off.
  • Denominator: loans outstanding at the start of the month: a balance above $10, not yet repossessed and not charged off. Loans already paid off or charged off do not count as outstanding.
  • Not a repossession: a flag on a loan that was current at the start of the month and paid in full during it. That is a payoff recorded in the wrong field. A borrower who is current but surrenders the vehicle is still counted, as is a delinquent borrower who redeems it by paying the loan off.
  • First month in our data: a flag already set when a deal first appears in our records is not counted — that repossession happened before our history of the deal begins.
  • Universe: loans with a consumer credit score of 300–900, as for delinquency; loans without a score, and commercial-scored loans, are excluded.
  • Filters: the credit-score range and new/used filters apply together, on 10-point score bands.

How the loan-level count compares with the servicer’s

Seven servicers print the number of vehicles repossessed each month in their 10-D reports. We compare it with the loan-level count every day; a shelf whose ratio moves is flagged for review.

ShelfLoan-level ÷ servicerReading
Capital One, Ford, Hyundai, Toyota≈ 1.0 Agree within a few percent; single months can differ by a month of timing.
Honda≈ 1.10 The difference matches the share of repossessions later reversed, which Honda’s report excludes and this site counts.
Santander (SDART, DRIVE)≈ 0.86 The loan-level file flags fewer repossessions than the servicer reports, steadily since 2017; the cause cannot be determined from public data.

Known breaks in the repossession flag

  • Exeter, from November 2022: the loan-level file largely stops setting the flag — first-time repossessions fall from 3,826 in October 2022 to 66 in November while charge-offs without a repossession rise in step. Exeter’s series ends at October 2022.
  • Ally, January–July 2020: the loan-level file flagged loan payoffs as repossessions (about 12,000 a month against about 450 normally). Within that window no flag on a loan paid in full that month is counted. Corrected, Ally’s 2020 rate is consistent with the 1.53% whole-portfolio repossession rate Ally published for 2020 (Ally Auto Receivables Trust 2022-1 prospectus).

A daily check flags any shelf whose repossession rate suddenly collapses or spikes, so a new break of this kind is caught in the month it appears.

6. Credit Score Methodology Auto Loan

Analysis uses the consumer credit bureau score reported in SEC ABS-EE filings, filtered to the 300–900 range. Seven of twenty-five issuers explicitly identify this as a FICO score; the remaining eighteen report a generic “bureau score” label. Industry practice strongly favors FICO Auto Score for auto lending decisions, but we cannot independently verify the score model for all issuers. Loans without a reported score (~0–15% by issuer) are excluded from the analysis.

Commercial / dealer bureau scores are excluded because they use a different scoring scale than consumer scores and would contaminate the low-score bands of prime issuers.

Scores are used as reported, on one scale. The site does not convert between score models, so a 640 from one issuer is not necessarily risk-equivalent to a 640 from another (see Known Limitations).

Carvana, 2026 deals: Carvana reports both a VantageScore and a FICO for most loans in its 2026 deals. The FICO is used where both are given, and the VantageScore where it is the only score; both are retained in our data.

7. Vintage Curves vs Time Series

  • Vintage Curves plot the metric as a function of months since deal issuance, one line per deal series. Useful for comparing the seasoning behavior of different vintages within the same issuer.
  • Time Series aggregates each issuer’s deals into a single calendar-time curve, one line per issuer. Useful for cross-issuer trend comparisons.

8. Scope & Boundary Notes

Auto Loan Issuers with commercial-auto exposure: for issuers that securitize both consumer and commercial auto loans (Ford, Ally), we exclude commercial-bureau-scored loans from our “Below Prime” bucket. Commercial bureau scores use a different scale than consumer FICO (typically 0–100), and including them would mix fleet and small-business loans with subprime consumer loans. For a consumer credit analysis, the stricter exclusion is more defensible.

Auto Loan Score boundary: our “Below Prime” bucket is scores 300–659 inclusive, reflecting 10-point score bucketing. Definitions that treat 660 as below prime will show slightly higher counts; the difference is typically small (∼1–4% for small-volume issuers where a repo happens to land on exactly 660).

Auto Lease Delinquency only (MVP): residual-value realization, disposition mix, and cumulative net loss metrics are computed by the pipeline but are not yet surfaced, pending independent corroboration.

9. Known Limitations

  • Auto Loan Repossession flag quality varies by issuer: the flag is only as good as each issuer’s file. Known breaks (Exeter from November 2022; Ally, January–July 2020) and how each shelf’s count compares with its servicer’s are set out in section 5.
  • Auto Loan Score model ambiguity: 18 of 25 issuers use a generic “bureau” label instead of explicitly naming FICO. A Vantage­Score 640 may not be risk-equivalent to a FICO 640.
  • Auto Loan Vehicle valuation varies by issuer: the reported vehicle value may reflect wholesale, retail, dealer purchase price, or another basis depending on the issuer’s servicing system. Cross-issuer LTV comparisons should be treated as directional, not precise.
  • Auto Loan Commercial purpose loans: some consumer-bureau-scored loans may have commercial use (e.g., sole proprietors financing work vehicles). The loan tape does not distinguish these, but visible evidence of distortion is minimal.
  • Auto Lease Entity-name aliasing: a handful of issuers (notably Ford) file under both their trust name and depositor name (e.g., “Ford Credit Auto Lease Two LLC”); these may appear as separate series in the legend with identical numbers.
  • Auto Lease No deal-specific triggers: pool-level performance against deal-specific covenants (delinquency triggers, residual loss triggers) requires prospectus-level data not included in ABS-EE.
  • Non-disclosed fields: SEC ABS-EE does not require disclosure of down payment amount, borrower income (only payment-to-income), employment status, housing, or dealer identity. These variables affect default risk but cannot be analyzed from this dataset.
